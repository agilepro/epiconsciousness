# Philippa Foot

An Oxford/UCLA philosopher (1920-2010) developed a distinctive theory of non-utilitarian naturalistic ethics in which the virtues play a central role.

## Hand Clasping

Foot says that the noncognitivist views (emotivism and prescriptivism) depend on two assumptions:

* what counts as evidence for an evaluative conclusion in no way depends on what anyone else counts as evidence.
* anyone can refuse to draw an evaluative conclusion in the face of what others regard as definitive evidence.

These assumptions lead to the conclusion that evaluative assumptions are entirely unconnected to factual judgements.  In other words: what is good has nothing to do with the fact of the matter.

To argue against the first assumption:  imagine Joe has been awarded the prize for best pumpkin, and feels pride in this accomplishment, but in fact it turns out that it was not his pumpkin.  We could no longer say he felt _pride_.   No matter his mental state, the facts of the matter no longer support feeling the emotion of _pride_.

It similarly would not be possible to feel proud of walking a single step, except that there is some special condition that made such walking impossible before, such as a severe injury.

“even feelings are logically vulnerable to facts”

This analogy extends to feeling that something is "good".  Foot asks us to consider someone who deems it good to always clasp one’s hands three times in an hour. This person might claim it is a duty to so clasp one’s hands, praise others for doing it and so on. Are these moral judgments?  Without some special reason for clasping hands, it can not be considered to be _good_.

To argue against the second assumption:  If an action can be shown to be in our interests, we have reason to do it, and so we can say we _ought_ to do it; it would be irrationally bad to not do it.  Cultivating virtues is in our interest, and therefor regardless of our present desires, it is _good_ to cultivate virtues.

Consider an injury that prevents the use of some significant part of the body.  We have reason to avoid this.  No rational person seeks an injury for itself, and every rational person avoids injury for itself.  General facts concerning human beings support the idea that it is necessary for rational people to want their limbs intact;  everyone _should_ avoid such injury.  If there was some reason that such an injury benefitted us, then would be good, and there is no reason we _should_ avoid it.

She argues that the virtues can be taken to be as essential to the fulfillment of our desires as are our limbs.  

## Virtues and Vices

This paper from 1978 is the title essay of the collection of her other writings.  The programmatic statement of modern virtue ethics: virtues as correctives to characteristic human temptations.  This is [discussed by Simon Cushing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gd5L_DWOx2M) on YouTube.

A virtue is a property of character.  Focus on moral virtues since there can be other kinds.  Three kinds: beneficial, will/intention, corrective.  You can't be moral by accident, intention is key but that is not enough, but the disposition of the heart.  Corrective is about being prepared to act the right way.

Utilitarianism can come into conflict with individual goods.  Justice sometimes appears to be against the common good.

Intentionally spelling something wrong, does not mean you are a bad speller, because it could be done artistically, but intentionally violating a virtue can never mean anything except you don't have the virtue.  Virtues are only virtues because of human weakness, corrective to human foibles.

Is the hero who was terrified more virtuous than the same act by someone who was not terrified?  But a better person might that who finds it easier.  It is possible to be courageous in doing evil, but in them courage is not a virtue.  Arsenic is a poison, but can be used to treat wood where it is not a poison.

### Outline of Virtues and Vices

I

* Virtues and vices have been neglected.
* Aristotle and Aquinas based morality on virtues/vices
* Some disagreement on definition of virtue/vice
* virtues are in some way beneficial to welfare.  (Just as good morals are beneficial)
* a virtue is something a person *needs* for themselves and their fellows
* only talking about intentional actions.  Accidents don't count.  Part of the will.
* innermost desires and will power.
* wisdom is an intellectual virtue.  Wisdom is how to act well to bring about good ends, and it presupposes good ends. 
* wisdom is on generally available experience, not specialized training.
* second part of wisdom is on values.
* how are virtues different from arts or skills?  A virtue is not just a capacity, but also it must engage the will.

II

* Courage and Temperance.   Courage faces fear, Temperance faces irrational desire.  Virtues are correctives against these basic feelings.
* Justice and Charity - don't fight against desire but fight against deficiency of motivation
* must be about things that are difficult to do. Courage appreciated only when it is difficult to be brave.
* avoiding stealing is more honorable in the person more tempted to steal
* charity is best found in the person for whom charity it easy
* courage can be about overcoming irrational fears, like claustrophobia or fear of heights
* Kant argues that only actions done of a sense of duty have positive moral worth.  A philanthropist who enjoys sharing is not really acting morally.
* Kant says self preservation is not worthy, except if out of duty
* There is no virtue in self-love.
* Rescuing Kant: charity is a virtue of attachment as well as action, so the sympathy that makes it easier to act with charity is part of the virtue itself.

III

* virtues are "a positively good action"
* what about when an action confirms one virtu, but contradicts another?
* Aquinas says a virtue can never be used for bad purpose (except when treated as objects)
* What about courage of the villain? (Peter Geach)
* Is charity stronger in a person who is willing to break the law for it.  (Robin Hood)
* How about the murder of a evil person?  Does that take courage?
* "we are inclined to say that it ‘took courage’; and yet it seems wrong to think of courage as equally connected with good actions and bad."
* the power of virtue is related to the power of producing good action (seems circular)
* " a disparity between the moral ideals that may seem to be implied in our talk about the virtues, and the moral judgements that we actually make"
* " For while wisdom always operates as a virtue, its close relation prudence does not, and it is prudence rather than wisdom that inspires many a careful life."
* "Nevertheless in some people temperance is not a virtue, but is rather connected with timidity or with a grudging attitude to the acceptance of good things. Of course what is best is to live boldly yet without imprudence or intemperance, but the fact is that rather few can manage that"

## What is a Virtue

This paragraph belings in a very general heading under ethics, however Phillippa Foot uses virtue so much, I need to assure there is a clear definition:

- **virtue** - a trait of moral excellence, goodness, or high character that guides a person to do what is right.
  - Good behavior: It means living a life that follows high moral standards and avoids wrong actions.
  - A habit: Virtue grows stronger when a person chooses to do good things consistently, even when it is hard.
- **vice** - opposite of virture, a bad or immoral habit.
- **defect** - instad of vice, Foot uses the word defect to describe when someone fails to do what is good.
- **conative** - mental processes related ot string, desire, volition, and purposeful goal directed action.  Thus, willing an action, as opposed to simply thinking about it.

An action is on a scale from good to bad, and a virtue is the tendency to do good actions.  A virtue can be a virtue only if it encourages good behavior, however it seems backward to say that an action is good *because* it was encouraged by a virtue.  The virtue is a virtue only *because* the actions it encourages are good ones.  If an action is bad, then we conclude it must have been motivated by a vice.  We must then judge the goodness of an action independently.

### 1. A Fresh Start

- evil is a kind of natural defect.
- rejects subjectivism, partisularly emotivism, prescriptivism, non-cognitivism as being based on a mistake
- all these are based on "linguistic philosophy" of the "evaluation" langage, and what that language is doing that is different from descriptive language.
- "courage" and "justice" describe but also carry moral meaning
- these theories are radically subjective, allowing for moral evaluation of any trivial act -- and consequently contradictions to be resolved.
- Instead something "conative" must be involved: the will to actually do the act that way.  The moral value of such a statement comes not from the evaluation statement, but instead the speaker's state of mind.
- these theories are devised to take advantage of the "action guiding" character of morality.  Hume says morality must guide action.
- The just person aims at keeping promises, but might not if something more relevant appears.  The claim about keeping promises by itself is not good enough to explain morality, because there are exceptions.  We need "practical rationality" to explain moral behavior.

## Main References

  1. "Moral Beliefs" (1958–59) — Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society 59(1): 83–104
  Her breakthrough. Attacks the fact/value gap by arguing that terms like "injury," "danger," and "harm" have built-in
  evaluative content that isn't up for grabs — you can't just decide that clasping your hands three times an hour is
  "good." This is the single most relevant paper to §1 of the critique.

  2. "Moral Arguments" (1958) — Mind 67(268): 502–513
  Argues against Hare that moral argument isn't endlessly open — there are limits on what can count as a reason for a
  moral conclusion.

  3. "Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives" (1972) — The Philosophical Review 81(3): 305–316
  Her most-cited article. Denies Kant's categorical imperative: moral reasons bind only those who already care about the
  ends morality serves. Read this one carefully — see the note below.

  4. "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect" (1967) — Oxford Review 5: 5–15
  The paper that invented the trolley problem (as a footnote to a larger argument about doing vs. allowing).

  5. "Virtues and Vices" (1978) — title essay of the collection
  The programmatic statement of modern virtue ethics: virtues as correctives to characteristic human temptations.  This is [discussed by Simon Cushing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gd5L_DWOx2M) on YouTube.

  6. "Euthanasia" (1977) — Philosophy and Public Affairs 6(2): 85–112
  Applies the killing/letting-die distinction and introduces her account of "life that is a good."

  7. "Moral Realism and Moral Dilemma" (1983) — The Journal of Philosophy 80(7): 379–398
  On whether genuine moral conflicts refute realism. Directly relevant to your book's chapter on rules conflicting.

  8. "Utilitarianism and the Virtues" (1985) — Mind 94(374): 196–209

  what-is-good.md:12.

  9. "Does Moral Subjectivism Rest on a Mistake?" (1995) — Oxford Journal of Legal Studies 15(1): 1–14

  10. "Rationality and Goodness" (2004) — in Modern Moral Philosophy, Royal Institute of Philosophy Supplement 54: 1–14
  Her late statement: practical rationality and moral goodness are not separable. Her last major paper.
