# Michael Huemer

Michael Huemer is a professor of moral philosophy at the University of Colorado and one of the leading proponents of moral realism: the thesis that there are true, objective moral facts.

Ethics is right and wrong. An evaluative statement is something like "murder is wrong."  Metaethics is why we think things could be right or wrong.  Five metaethical theories

- **Moral Realists**, there are objective evaluative truths
   - Reductionist/**Naturalists**: says that there is a reason that something is right/wrong without needing any person's feeling to be involved.  You can explain what is wrong with an act in objective terms.  Explained without evaluative arguments.  Empirical or scientific explanations give us epistemic knowledge.
   - Not Reducible/**Intuitionists**:  Can not reduce to evaluative truths.  And so you must use evaluative language to define right and wrong.  Intuition just IS what there is.  Believe there are facts about things beyond the descriptive facts, and there is no epistemic knowledge.  Standard religious dualists fall in this category along with divine command theory.
- **Antirealists**, there are no objective evaluative truths
   - **Expressivists** / **Non-cognitivists**: Statements are neither true nor false.  Such statements are not making assertions, they are just expressions of your feelings about things.  "booo murder".
   - **Nihilist** / **Error Theorists**:  Evaluative statement are always false.  There is no good/bad right/wrong.  You apply a property to something that can have no such property.  Positive statements false.
   - **Subjectivists**: there are evaluative truths, but it depends on observer. Something is true because our culture believes it is good/bad.


Two issues: epistemological issue about "how we know about moral truths" and the metaphysical issue "whether they are reducible".

Dualism vs Monism:  Monism is one kind of facts, Dualism is two kinds of facts.

- **Prescriptivism** - when you make moral statements you are commanding people.  It is about influencing behavior
- **Emotivism** - moral claims are expressions of attitudes, not beliefs.  "booo murder"  Expressing theorisms.

Students get confused between subjectivism (describing your feelings) and emotivism (expressing your feelings).  "I wonder whether murder is wrong" makes no sense for emotivist.

### My responses

- "Naturalist says you can explain moral truth using non evaluative language."  That is correct: behaviors that lead to survival are good.  Take two tribes, each take a position on a moral issue, and find out which one lives the longest.
- "Seemings are all we have." Disagree.  Survival is a hard fact.  If we can point to a behavior that increases the chance of survival, that is invariably an ethically good thing to do.  Similarly something that decreases survival is bad.
- "Intuitionism is unable to account for how we know what it moral." - however survival of the fittest will select for those people who "know" these things, and eliminate those who do not. That explains it.
- "Maybe maximizing reproduction is good, but nobody believes that."  Not your own personal reproduction but instead maximizing total size of tribe and ability to propagate to a new generation.
- How do we "know" the moral truth.  Existence and knowledge is not the same thing.  Moral truths might exist without anyone knowing them.
- "Super hard to explain how evolution can do this."  Not really, either people are wired to know a particular moral intuition, or they don't survive.
- "Take a question about pleasure, and substitute good for pleasure, and notice the meaning changes."  Pleasure is unrelated to good.   Good is things that increase survival.  The pleasure is simply something that evolution generated when doing the right things, so that those people service more often, and this trait is inherited.  But the act is not good _because_ it is pleasurable, it is pleasurable because that pleasure led people to doing the good thing.
- Epistemic access: we don't know most of the real moral rules.
- "Actual moral facts don't effect your reproductive success." Disagree. Morals exist specifically to increase reproductive success.  Being greedy might seem like a benefit, but in the long term it isn't, so averaging over everyone, more people survive in a sharing environment.
- "pain is bad can't be justified on moral naturalism."  It is not the pain that is bad.  The pain just comes along for the ride.  Almost all things that are painful are things that correlate strongly to things that endanger you.



## Questions

- It seems that people expect morality to be phrased like "XXXXX is good" where XXXXX is a description of an action.  This evaluative statement is then assumed to be true for all times, all contexts, all people.
- What is the justification for the assumption that such a moral rule will apply to all people equally?  Isn't it possible that some action is good for one person, but bad for another.
- What justification can be given for this evaluation to be static over time?  Can't something be moral today, but immoral tomorrow?
- To make any statement about a moral action, you need to define the action.  A short description of a few words can't possibly define it well enough.  "lying" or "telling a falsehood" is a broad range of occasions many of which are not only acceptable, but in cases absolutely required.  "Tact" is the name for lying when it is appropriate.  If the definition of the action is fuzzy, how then can you reliably associate a moral valence?  Shooting someone in self defense is different than shooting a premeditated intentional kill for material gain.  
- Is the morality in the act, or in the statement about the act.  So much of this discussion seem to focus on whether evaluative statements are "true" but isn't that besides the point?  We want to know if a particular act was moral, such as, say, the killing of Louis XVI.  How can we say any particular statement about morality would apply to that situation?
- how specific should a moral statement be?  There is some desire to make them succinct: "lying is evil" or something like that.  There are all sorts of exceptions that are part of common sense that are not included in the statement.
